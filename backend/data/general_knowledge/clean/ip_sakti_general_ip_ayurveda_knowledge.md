1. IP BASICS
What is Intellectual Property (IP)?
Intellectual Property (IP) means legal rights given to creations of the human mind—such as inventions, brand
names, designs, literary/artistic works, and certain traditional knowledge—so the creator/owner can control
use and get benefit for a limited time.
IP turns intangible creations into legally protectable assets. In India, main IP types are patents, trademarks,
geographical indications (GI), copyright, designs, trade secrets (via contract/common law), and protection for
traditional knowledge (e.g., via TKDL and patent law defences).
 IP is territorial: Indian IP rights generally protect only in India unless you file abroad or use international systems.
 Different IP types protect different subject matter and have different durations and formalities.
 IP can be licensed, sold (assigned), or used as security, like other property.
The Patents Act, 1970; The Trade Marks Act, 1999; The Copyright Act, 1957; The Designs Act, 2000; The Geographical
Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999.
Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs & Trade Marks (CGPDTM), IP India (DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce &
Industry).
IP India portal (Patents/Trade Marks/Designs/GI/Copyright resources)
https://ipindia.gov.in/
This is a high■level definition. Specific eligibility, term, and enforcement depend on the exact IP type and facts.
What are the main types of IP in India?
The main types of IP in India are: patents (inventions), trademarks (brand signs), geographical indications
(origin■linked products), copyright (literary/artistic works), designs (visual appearance of articles), trade
secrets (confidential business information), and plant variety protection (new plant varieties).

Each type protects different subject matter. For example, a new herbal formulation may be patentable if it
meets patent criteria; its brand name can be a trademark; its packaging shape/look may be a design; the
underlying text/images in labels are copyright; and if the formulation is traditional, TKDL may treat it as prior
art.
 Patents: technical inventions (products/processes).
 Trademarks: words, logos, sounds, shapes that distinguish goods/services.
 GI: names linking product quality/reputation to a geographical origin.
 Copyright: original literary, dramatic, musical, artistic works, films, sound recordings, software.
 Designs: visual appearance (shape, pattern, ornamentation) of articles.
 Trade secrets: confidential formulas, processes, customer lists (protected via contracts and common law).
 Plant Variety Protection: new plant varieties under PPV&FR; Act.
Patents Act 1970; Trade Marks Act 1999; Copyright Act 1957; Designs Act 2000; GI Act 1999; PPV&FR; Act 2001.

2. PATENTS
What is a patent in India?
A patent is a government■granted right that gives the inventor/owner the exclusive right to prevent others
from making, using, selling, or importing the patented invention in India for up to 20 years from filing, subject
to paying renewal fees.
A patent protects technical inventions (products or processes) that are new, involve an inventive step, and
are industrially applicable. In exchange, the inventor discloses the invention in a published specification.
 Term: 20 years from filing date (for most patents).
 Rights: Exclude others from commercial exploitation of the claimed invention.
 Territorial: Indian patent protects only in India.
Step■by■Step Process
1. Check patentability and prior art.
2. File application (Form 1 + specification: Form 2).
3. Publish (automatic at 18 months or early on request).
4. Request examination (Form 18).
5. Respond to examination report; attend hearing if needed.
6. Grant → pay renewals from 3rd year onwards.
The Patents Act, 1970 (as amended up to 01■08■2024); Patents Rules 2003 (as amended 2024).
Indian Patent Office (CGPDTM), under DPIIT.
IP India – Patents: Before You Apply / Filing Process; Patents Act & Rules
https://ipindia.gov.in/
This does not determine patentability of any specific invention; that requires claim■by■claim analysis against prior art
and exclusions (e.g., Section 3 of Patents Act).
What can and cannot be patented in India?

You can patent new, inventive, industrially applicable inventions (products or processes). You cannot patent
mere discoveries, scientific theories, business methods 'as such', algorithms 'as such', methods of treatment,
plants/animals in essence, and inventions contrary to public order/morality, among others.
Patentability has positive criteria (novelty, inventive step, industrial application) and negative exclusions
(Section 3 & 5 of Patents Act). Even if something is new, it may still be non■patentable if it falls under
excluded categories.
 Must be an 'invention': a manner of manufacture, product, or process.
 Must not fall under Section 3 exclusions (e.g., frivolous, contrary to law, mere discovery, traditional knowledge per se,
etc.).
 Pharmaceutical/chemical products are patentable subject to other criteria (post■2005 regime).
The Patents Act, 1970 – Sections 2(1)(j), 3, 5.
Indian Patent Office; Courts interpreting Patents Act.

legal/technical analysis.
Who can file a patent application in India?
The true and first inventor, or their assignee/legal representative (including employers for employee
inventions under certain conditions), can file. Foreign applicants can file via Indian patent agents and must
provide an Indian address for service.
Ownership and right to apply depend on who invented and any assignment/employment agreements. For
PCT national phase entries, the applicant under PCT can enter India within prescribed time limits.
 Inventor(s) must be named; applicant may be inventor or assignee.
 Joint inventors/applicants allowed.
 Foreign applicants require Indian patent agent for filing and an address for service in India.

The Patents Act, 1970 – Sections 6, 7, 134; Patents Rules 2003.
Indian Patent Office.

3. TRADEMARKS
What is a trademark in India?
A trademark is a sign (word, logo, label, shape, colour combination, sound, etc.) that distinguishes goods or
services of one business from another. Registration gives stronger legal protection and a presumption of
ownership.
Trademarks protect brand identity, not the product itself. Registration is not strictly mandatory to use a mark,
but it greatly helps in enforcement and licensing.
 Valid for 10 years from filing, renewable indefinitely.
 Can be filed for one or more Nice classes of goods/services.
 Rights are territorial (India■only unless filed abroad/Madrid).
Step■by■Step Process
1. Search existing marks (tmrsearch.ipindia.gov.in).
2. File Form TM■A (e■filing) with mark, class(es), and fee.
3. Examination → acceptance/refusal.
4. Publication in Trade Marks Journal.
5. Opposition window (4 months).
6. Registration (if no opposition/successful defence).
The Trade Marks Act, 1999; Trade Marks Rules, 2017.
Trade Marks Registry (CGPDTM), IP India.

https://ipindia.gov.in/
Does not determine registrability of a specific mark (distinctiveness, conflict, descriptiveness must be assessed
case■by■case).
What can be registered as a trademark?

Any mark capable of being represented graphically and capable of distinguishing goods/services can be
registered: words, logos, labels, numerals, shapes, packaging, colour combinations, sounds, etc., subject to
distinctiveness and non■conflict with earlier marks.
The mark must not be generic/descriptive for the goods/services, must not deceive or cause confusion, and
must not fall under prohibited categories (e.g., official emblems without permission).
 Distinctiveness is key (inherent or acquired through use).
 Descriptive or common terms may be refused unless they have acquired secondary meaning.
 Well■known marks get broader protection across classes.
The Trade Marks Act, 1999 – Sections 2, 9, 11.
Trade Marks Registry; Courts.

4. GEOGRAPHICAL INDICATIONS (GI)
What is a Geographical Indication (GI)?
A GI is a sign used on goods that have a specific geographical origin and possess qualities, reputation, or
characteristics essentially due to that origin (e.g., Darjeeling Tea, Nagpur Orange).
GI protects the link between product and place, preventing misuse of the name by producers outside the
region. It is typically owned by a producer association or competent authority, not a single company.
 Applies to agricultural, natural, manufactured, handicraft goods originating from a defined territory.
 Registration is for 10 years, renewable.
 Helps rural/artisan communities and exports.
Step■by■Step Process
1. Prepare GI application with details of territory, product, link between quality/reputation and origin.
2. File with GI Registry (Form as per GI Rules).
3. Examination → acceptance → publication.
4. Opposition window.
5. Registration → authorised users can be added.
Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999; GI Rules 2002 as amended 2025.
GI Registry (CGPDTM), IP India.

5. COPYRIGHT
What is copyright in India?
Copyright is a legal right that protects original literary, dramatic, musical, artistic works, cinematograph films,
and sound recordings from unauthorized copying, distribution, adaptation, or public communication.
Copyright arises automatically when an original work is fixed in a tangible form; registration is optional but
gives strong evidence in court. It does not protect ideas, only their expression.
 Term: generally life of author + 60 years (for literary, dramatic, musical, artistic); 60 years from publication for films,
sound recordings, etc.
 Registration via Form XIV on copyright.gov.in; 30■day objection window.
 Software is protected as a literary work.
Step■by■Step Process
1. Create account on copyright.gov.in.
2. File Form XIV with work details and upload soft copy.
3. Pay fee (■500–■5,000 depending on category).
4. Wait 30 days for objections; then examination.
5. Receive registration certificate (typically 8–14 months in uncontested cases).
The Copyright Act, 1957; Copyright Rules, 2013 (Second Schedule – fees).
Copyright Office, Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT).
Copyright Registration in India 2026 — Form XIV, Fees (guide citing Rules); Copyright Office
https://copyright.gov.in/
Registration is not mandatory for protection; this answer focuses on registration process/fees.

6. INDUSTRIAL DESIGNS
What is a design right in India?
A design right protects the visual appearance (shape, configuration, pattern, ornamentation) of an article, not
its functional features. Registration gives exclusive rights to apply the design to the article for up to 15 years
(initial 10 + extension 5).
Designs protect how a product looks, not how it works. Functional features may be patentable instead.
Design registration is useful for consumer products, packaging, UI icons (if applicable), etc.
 Term: 10 years from registration, extendable by 5 more years.
 Must be new/original and not dictated solely by function.
 Classified under Locarno Classification.
Step■by■Step Process
1. Search existing designs (optional but recommended).
2. File Form 1 (application for registration of design) with representations of the design.
3. Examination → acceptance/refusal.
4. Registration → renew after 10 years (if desired).
The Designs Act, 2000; Designs Rules, 2001 as amended by Designs (Amendment) Rules 2021.
Designs Office (CGPDTM), IP India.
Designs Rules 2001 as amended 2021 (First Schedule); IP India
https://ipindia.gov.in/
Does not cover all design■related forms/fees (e.g., certified copies, inspection); consult First Schedule for full list.

7. TRADE SECRETS & PLANT VARIETY PROTECTION
What is a trade secret?
A trade secret is confidential business information (e.g., formulas, processes, customer lists) that gives a
competitive advantage and is protected through secrecy measures and contracts, rather than registration.
India does not have a standalone trade secret statute; protection comes from contract law, confidentiality
agreements, and common law actions for breach of confidence.
 No registration; protection depends on reasonable secrecy measures.
 Often used for formulations, manufacturing processes, algorithms, business data.
 Can coexist with patents (e.g., patent some aspects, keep others as trade secrets).
Contract Act 1872; common law principles; IT Act 2000 (for electronic records).
Courts; parties via contracts.
General legal principles; no single IP statute.

What is Plant Variety Protection (PVP) in India?
Plant Variety Protection gives breeders exclusive rights over new plant varieties that are novel, distinct,
uniform, and stable. It is governed by the PPV&FR; Act, 2001.
PVP encourages development of new plant varieties while protecting farmers' rights to save and use seeds
under certain conditions.
 Administered by PPV&FR; Authority, Ministry of Agriculture.
 Covers extant, new, and farmers' varieties under defined conditions.

 Separate from patents; plant varieties per se are not patentable under Section 3(h) of Patents Act.
Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights (PPV&FR;) Act, 2001.

8. TRADITIONAL KNOWLEDGE / TKDL
What is Traditional Knowledge (TK) in the Indian IP context?
Traditional Knowledge (TK) refers to knowledge, innovations, and practices of indigenous and local
communities, often developed over generations, especially in areas like medicine, agriculture, and
biodiversity. In IP, TK is important for preventing wrongful patents on existing traditional remedies and
practices.
TK itself is generally not patented as such, but it can block patents that try to claim something already known
in traditional systems. India uses TK defensively, mainly through the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library
(TKDL).
 TK includes Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Sowa Rigpa, Yoga formulations and practices.
 TK is documented in classical texts and oral traditions.
 TKDL translates and classifies TK into patent■friendly formats for international examiners.
Patents Act provisions on novelty/inventive step (used with TKDL as prior art).
CSIR■TKDL Unit, in collaboration with Ministry of AYUSH; Patent Offices (India and foreign).
TKDL Access Agreements; PIB/CSIR press releases; TKDL documentation.
https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2241127 ;
https://www.csir.res.in/en/press-release/india-and-brazil-sign-tkdl-access- agreement-strengthen-protection-traditional
This does not create a standalone 'TK right' for communities; it mainly prevents misappropriation via patent examination
and oppositions.
What is TKDL (Traditional Knowledge Digital Library)?
TKDL is a digital database of Indian traditional medicinal knowledge (Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Sowa Rigpa,
Yoga) created by CSIR and Ministry of AYUSH to prevent wrongful patents by making TK visible as prior art
to patent examiners worldwide.

TKDL translates and classifies traditional formulations into multiple languages and patent classification
systems, allowing examiners at 18+ patent offices to find TK during search and reject non■novel claims.
CSIR■TKDL also files third■party observations/oppositions using TKDL evidence.
 Established in 2001 by CSIR + Ministry of AYUSH.
 Contains over 5.2 lakh medicinal formulations/practices, translated into English, German, French, Japanese, Spanish,
etc.
 Access agreements with multiple foreign patent offices under non■disclosure terms.
CSIR■TKDL Access Agreements; Patents Act (novelty/inventive step).
CSIR■TKDL Unit; Ministry of AYUSH; participating international patent offices.
CSIR press releases on TKDL access agreements; PIB

https://www.csir.res.in/en/press- release/india-and-australia-sign-agreement-access-council-scientific-and-

TKDL is a defensive tool, not a registration system that grants positive rights to communities.

9. ABS / BIODIVERSITY
What is ABS (Access and Benefit Sharing) under India's Biodiversity
law?
ABS means the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the use of biological resources and
associated traditional knowledge, as required under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (as amended 2023)
and Rules.
If you access Indian biological resources (or associated TK) for research, commercial use, or IPR filing, you
may need NBA approval and to share benefits (monetary/non■monetary) with providers/local communities.
 Applies to biological resources and associated TK accessed in India.
 NBA (National Biodiversity Authority) grants approvals for certain categories; State Biodiversity Boards handle others.
 IPR applicants must disclose source and comply with ABS before grant in many cases.
Step■by■Step Process
1. Determine if your activity involves Indian biological resources/associated TK.
2. Check if NBA or State Board approval is needed (based on applicant type and purpose).
3. File required ABS application (Form■I etc.) and obtain approval.
4. Comply with benefit■sharing terms (agreements, payments, etc.).
Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (as amended 2023); Biological Diversity Rules, 2004 (as amended).
National Biodiversity Authority (NBA); State Biodiversity Boards.
NBA portal; PIB notifications on ABS
https://nbaindia.nic.in/
This is a general overview; specific ABS obligations depend on resource type, user category, and state rules.

10. PRIOR ART / PATENTABILITY BASICS
What is prior art?
Prior art is all information made available to the public before the filing/priority date of a patent application that
is relevant to assessing novelty and inventive step.
Prior art includes earlier patents, published applications, journal articles, products, public uses, and traditional
knowledge (including TKDL). If an invention is fully disclosed in prior art, it lacks novelty.
 Used to test novelty (same invention already known?) and inventive step (obvious to skilled person?).
 TKDL is treated as prior art for traditional medicinal knowledge.
 Non■patent literature and public uses can also be prior art.
Patents Act 1970 – Sections 2(1)(l), 13, 25 (opposition grounds).
Indian Patent Office; Courts.
Patents Act 1970; IP India examination guidelines
https://www.ipindia.gov.in/resource/patents-resources-act
Prior art analysis is fact■specific and technical; this is a conceptual overview.
What are novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability?
These are the three core patentability criteria: novelty means the invention is not already known; inventive
step means it is not obvious to a person skilled in the art; industrial applicability means it can be made or
used in an industry.
All three must be satisfied for a patent. An invention can be new but obvious (lacks inventive step), or
non■obvious but not industrially applicable.
 Novelty: no single prior art discloses all features of the claim.

 Inventive step: technical advance or economic significance, not obvious to skilled person.
 Industrial applicability: capable of industrial production/use.
Patents Act 1970 – Section 2(1)(j), (ja), (ac).

11. PROCESSES (PATENT, TM, GI, COPYRIGHT, DESIGN)
What is the patent process in India (simplified)?
The patent process involves filing an application, publication, examination, responding to objections, and
grant, followed by annual renewals. Timelines vary; examination is not automatic and must be requested.
Most applications take several years from filing to grant, depending on field, backlog, and whether expedited
examination is used.
 File Form 1 + Form 2 (specification).
 Publication at 18 months (or early on Form 9).
 Request examination (Form 18) within prescribed time.
 Respond to examination report; attend hearing if needed.
 Grant → renew from 3rd year.

What is the trademark registration process in India (simplified)?
The trademark process involves filing, examination, publication, possible opposition, and registration. If no
opposition is filed, registration typically occurs within about a year or so, subject to objections.
Objections on absolute/relative grounds are common; responses must address distinctiveness and conflict
with earlier marks.

12. PRIOR■ART SEARCH BASICS
How do I do a basic prior■art search for patents in India?
Start with keyword and classification searches on free databases like IP India's patent search, Google
Patents, and WIPO PATENTSCOPE. Look for similar products/processes, especially in your technical field.
For Ayurveda/herbal formulations, also consider TKDL (via examiners) and published traditional texts as
potential prior art.
 Use keywords in English and relevant Indian languages where possible.
 Use IPC/CPC classifications to narrow results.
 Check both Indian and international patents/publications.
 Document search strategy and key references for your records.
Step■by■Step Process
1. Define technical problem and key features of your invention.
2. Identify keywords and classifications.
3. Search IP India patent database, Google Patents, PATENTSCOPE.
4. Review top results for similarity to your claims.
5. Note relevant documents for novelty/inventive step analysis.
Patents Act 1970 (novelty/inventive step).
Self■conduct search; Patent Office conducts formal search during examination.
IP India patent search; Google Patents; WIPO PATENTSCOPE

13. AYURVEDA■SPECIFIC IP QUESTIONS
Can an Ayurvedic formulation be patented in India?
An Ayurvedic formulation can be patented only if it qualifies as a patentable invention: it must be new, involve
an inventive step, and be industrially applicable, and must not fall under exclusions like 'traditional knowledge
per se' or mere discovery.
If the formulation is already described in classical texts or TKDL, it likely lacks novelty or is treated as TK,
making patent protection difficult. New combinations, improved processes, or specific standardized extracts
may still be patentable if they meet all criteria.
 Classical formulations known in TK are generally not patentable as such.
 Novel combinations, new dosage forms, or improved manufacturing processes may be patentable.
 Patentability requires claim■level analysis against prior art and Section 3 exclusions.
Patents Act 1970 – Sections 2(1)(j), (ja), 3 (especially 3(d), 3(p)).
Indian Patent Office; Courts.
Patents Act 1970; IP India examination guidelines; TKDL documentation
https://www.ipindia.gov.in/resource/patents-resources-act
This is general guidance; each formulation requires technical/legal assessment of claims and prior art.
Should I protect an Ayurvedic product by patent, trademark, design, or
copyright?
It depends on what you want to protect: the technical invention (patent), the brand name/logo (trademark),
the packaging/shape (design), and the label text/images (copyright). Often, a combination is used.
For example, a new standardized extract process may be patented; the brand name is trademarked; the
bottle shape is registered as a design; the label artwork and brochure are copyrighted.

 Patent: new formulation/process with technical advance.
 Trademark: brand name, logo, tagline.
 Design: unique packaging shape/pattern.
 Copyright: label text, images, website content, brochures.
Step■by■Step Process
1. Identify what aspects are technical vs branding vs visual vs content.
2. Check patentability of technical aspects (novelty, inventive step).
3. File trademark for brand elements in relevant classes.
4. Consider design registration for distinctive packaging.
5. Rely on automatic copyright for content; optionally register.

14. AYUSH REGULATORY BASICS
How are Ayurvedic products classified for regulation in India?
Ayurvedic products can be classified as drugs (ASU drugs), cosmetics, or food/nutraceuticals depending on
ingredients, claims, and intended use. The regulatory pathway (license, approvals, labeling) depends on this
classification.
For example, a classical Ayurvedic medicine listed in authoritative texts is regulated as an ASU drug under
Drugs & Cosmetics Act/Rules and AYUSH notifications; a herbal hair oil making only cosmetic claims may be
a cosmetic; a herbal supplement making health■support claims may fall under FSSAI as food/nutraceutical.
 ASU drugs: regulated by Ministry of AYUSH and State Licensing Authorities under Drugs & Cosmetics Act/Rules.
 Cosmetics: regulated under Drugs & Cosmetics Act/Rules (cosmetics provisions).
 Food/nutraceuticals: regulated by FSSAI under FSS Act, 2006.
Step■by■Step Process
1. List all ingredients and their sources.
2. Check intended use and claims (therapeutic vs cosmetic vs nutritional).
3. Map to ASU drug / cosmetic / food category.
4. Follow relevant licensing/approval pathway (AYUSH/State Drug Control/FSSAI).
Drugs & Cosmetics Act 1940 & Rules 1945 (ASU drugs); Cosmetics Rules 2020; Food Safety and Standards Act 2006 &
Regulations.
Ministry of AYUSH; State Drug Control Authorities; FSSAI.
Ministry of AYUSH; FSSAI; Drugs & Cosmetics Act/Rules
https://ayush.gov.in/ ; https://fssai.gov.in/
Classification can be complex; borderline products need careful legal/regulatory review.
Do I need a licence to manufacture an Ayurvedic medicine in India?

Yes. Manufacturing Ayurvedic (ASU) drugs requires a licence from the State Licensing Authority under the
Drugs & Cosmetics Act/Rules and applicable AYUSH notifications, along with compliance with GMP and
other requirements.
Licence requirements vary for classical vs proprietary medicines and by state. Some categories may also
require product permissions or approvals.
 Classical medicines: based on authoritative texts; specific schedule requirements.
 Proprietary medicines: new combinations/forms; may need additional scrutiny.
 GMP compliance and qualified technical staff are mandatory.

16. IP COMPARISONS
What is the difference between patent, trademark, GI, copyright, and
design?
These IP types protect different things: patents protect technical inventions; trademarks protect brand signs;
GI protects origin■linked product names; copyright protects original works of authorship; designs protect
visual appearance of articles.
They differ in subject matter, duration, registration need, and what rights they give.
 Patent: invention; up to 20 years; registration mandatory for protection.
 Trademark: brand sign; 10■yearly renewable; registration optional but strongly recommended.
 GI: geographical name for products; 10■yearly renewable; registration needed for GI right.
 Copyright: original works; life+60 or 60 years; automatic, registration optional.
 Design: appearance of article; 10+5 years; registration needed for design right.

18. INDIA VS INTERNATIONAL IP BASICS
How does Indian IP protection compare with international systems?
India follows major international IP treaties (e.g., Paris, PCT, Berne, TRIPS), so basic principles are similar,
but procedures, fees, and some substantive rules differ by country.
For patents, you can file in India directly or via PCT national phase. For trademarks, you can use Madrid
Protocol for international registration designating India. Copyright protection is largely automatic across Berne
Union countries.
 Patents: PCT allows single international filing, but grant is national/regional.
 Trademarks: Madrid System allows multi■country registration via one application.
 Copyright: automatic in most countries; registration mainly evidentiary.
 GI: India has its own GI Act; other countries may use sui generis or trademark■based systems.
Patents Act 1970 (PCT provisions); Trade Marks Act 1999 (Madrid); Copyright Act 1957 (Berne).
